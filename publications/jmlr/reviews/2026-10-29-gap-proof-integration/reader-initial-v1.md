## What I understand the claims to be

The local lemma controls one estimated action gap, measured against the action selected by a reference policy. The competing action is genuinely worse under that policy's actual action values. If the estimated gap is positive at a finite restart, the chance that it becomes nonpositive by the next change of the actual action-value target, or by exit from a fixed estimate bound, is at most a constant times the largest remaining learning rate divided by the starting gap, plus a constant times the remaining sum of squared learning rates. The update ending at the stopping endpoint is included. This is a bound for one gap, not a claim that no gap can ever close or that every policy transition improves.

The assembled result is that initial-visit MC-O-PI converges to the optimal action values under the standing assumptions. Its action-value targets change only finitely often almost surely. Policies may still change among optimal ties, so eventual policy constancy is asserted only when each state has a unique optimal action.

Uniformity is within a state: `sigma_k(s)` is the probability of each action pair there, not the probability of selecting the state itself. The latter is the number of available actions times that quantity. States can be sampled at unequal adaptive frequencies and can wait arbitrarily long, subject to infinite accumulated learning at each pair.

## The local mechanism, in my own words

Before the target changes, a bad competitor's estimated gap is pulled toward a fixed positive target gap of at least `4 delta`. Both this pull and the variance of its noise carry the same state update probability. This shared factor is the reason the analysis can tolerate long waits: it measures how much expected learning the state has accumulated, rather than relying on elapsed iterations alone.

The proof stops the noise sum at the first closed gap or the interval endpoint. The increment that produces that first closed gap is retained because an update from iteration `k` ends at `k+1`; the test `k < eta` therefore retains the increment ending at `eta`. Conditional zero mean survives this stopping, since the decision whether to retain the current increment is already known before drawing the episode. The same argument supplies the second-moment bound.

There are two exhaustive cases for the first nonpositive gap:

1. **It closes without having reached `delta`.** Up to closure it is between zero and `delta`, so every mean increment is at least `3 delta` times the state's learning increment. Noise must erase the starting gap and all this accumulated gain. Thus the required downward fluctuation grows with accumulated learning: `b_n = d_start + 3 delta A_n`. Dividing each noise increment by its corresponding predictable threshold produces `Z`. This is not the same as simply dividing the final noise sum by `b_n`; the summation-by-parts identity supplies the needed link. Since the thresholds grow, their difference weights are nonnegative and their total is at most the final threshold. Therefore `|M_n| <= 2 b_n max |Z_m|`. Erasing `b_n` forces `Z` to reach magnitude at least one half. A maximal inequality then bounds this event by four times the expected sum of squared rescaled increments.

   The rescaled variance has the form `bar alpha` times a learning increment divided by the square of its right-endpoint threshold. The decreasing-function comparison sums these terms below an integral. Its value is proportional to `1 / (delta d_start)`, producing the first term of the lemma. No divergence of the accumulated learning is needed for this upper bound: extending a finite collection of learning intervals to the entire positive half-line only enlarges it.

2. **It first reaches `delta`, then closes.** Use the last time it was at least `delta` before the first closure. The noise-free next update remains at least `delta`, because it is a convex combination of the current gap and a target of at least `4 delta`. Subsequent gaps before closure lie below `delta` and have nonnegative drift. Noise must therefore have fallen by at least `delta` between that last high gap and closure. At least one of the two noise-sum values has magnitude `delta/2`. The ordinary stopped noise sum suffices here; its total second moment is bounded by the squared-learning-rate tail. The last-high-gap index is a pathwise device, not a new time at which the proof conditions on future information.

Adding the two event bounds yields the lemma. The two cases are useful because the starting gap may be arbitrarily small, whereas reaching `delta` makes a fixed noise threshold available. The first estimate addresses the small-gap difficulty; the second handles later loss of a substantial gap.

### Why the small starting gap does not invalidate the probability step

The rescaled noise need not be globally square-integrable just because its denominator is positive. The proof temporarily restricts to starting histories where that denominator is at least a fixed epsilon. This bounds the squared rescaled increments by a deterministic multiple of `alpha_k^2`. The maximal inequality is then justified on those histories. A countable collection of such restrictions covers all positive starting gaps, so the final inequality contains no uniform positive-gap restriction. Fixed-time conditional bounds are obtained by testing on events known at the restart; random finite restarts are handled on their separate integer-valued slices. Increasing finite horizons gives the event of a crossing at some finite future time. I can follow these reductions; they are doing different jobs and are not interchangeable.

## How the local lemma feeds the later argument

At a new reference policy, some bad-action gaps can be zero. Seeding supplies a margin without requiring the state to be sampled within a bounded number of iterations. A small bad-action gap means either the reference estimate is sufficiently too low or the competitor estimate is sufficiently too high. A favorable return when updating the appropriate action corrects that error enough to increase the intended gap by more than `delta alpha_k`. These updates cannot reduce any other gap against the reference action at that state. Greedy inertia retains the reference action during successful requests and during waits.

Each state works through a finite list independently. A processed gap either reaches `delta` or receives `N` favorable increases. The comparison condition on learning rates makes every such earlier increase at least a fixed fraction of the largest rate remaining at that state's completion. Thus its completion margin is at least the smaller of `delta` and `N delta bar alpha / c_alpha`. The seed lemma bounds the number of requests, not the elapsed duration.

The progress proof must avoid subtracting a polynomially small failure bound from the exponentially small probability of successful seeds. It also cannot casually condition the original law on successful seeding: later updates at completed states can affect which future requests occur. Instead it uses an auxiliary law that forces requests to succeed while preserving the state-selection probabilities and the updates at completed states. The appendix's bounded likelihood weights explain why future adaptive requests average out and why the original probability of any desired future event is at least the auxiliary probability times the fixed seed factor.

Under this auxiliary law, completed gaps are eligible for the preservation bound. Choose `N` large enough to make the ratio term small, then the start late enough to make the squared-rate tail small. A union bound makes the chance of any completed bad gap closing at most one half. No independence or common completion time is required. Unfinished states are safe by inertia; states completed exactly at the target-change endpoint also retain their reference action. At a finite transition before exit, avoiding all bad competitors gives the policy-improvement inequality at every state. A target change supplies the difference in action values needed for strict improvement. If the endpoint is infinite without exit, the target is already constant, so all states need not complete.

Transfer back gives a fixed positive chance of either stopping or strict improvement per continuing interval. The global proof needs consecutive improvements, not monotonicity of the actual noisy policy sequence. Among finitely many policies a sufficiently long consecutive improvement chain is impossible. From each surviving interval, a block therefore has a uniform positive chance to stop. Repeated blocks make infinite continuation probability zero even when failed transitions worsen the policy. Boundedness removes exits by taking probability-one statements for every deterministic restart, then choosing a sufficiently late restart separately on each path. Once the target is constant, the fixed-target averaging result and a policy recurring infinitely often show that the limiting target is its own greedy policy's action values, hence optimal.

## Essential assumptions and their jobs

- Finite states/actions/policies give a positive minimum bad-action target gap, a finite seed budget, a finite union bound, and a finite bound on consecutive strict improvements.
- Discounting or properness gives finite policy values and the policy-improvement/optimality facts used throughout.
- Initial visits and action uniformity give the particular gap drift and variance factors. They also allow a specified favorable action with a fixed conditional probability once its state is selected.
- Conditional unbiasedness and bounded return variance supply the noise estimates and a positive one-sided favorable-return probability.
- Rates at most one make the noise-free update a convex combination. Square summability makes the noise tails and localized second moments finite and small at late times. The weakened monotonicity/comparison condition turns a finite seed budget into a margin proportional to future rates. The divergence of accumulated learning is needed for fixed-target convergence, rather than for the local preservation integral bound.
- Greedy inertia protects unfinished states and states not updated; greediness alone would permit unnecessary tie switches there.
- The estimate cutoff supplies deterministic variance bounds during each interval. Eventual boundedness is applied after the local estimates; the proof does not condition on future boundedness.
