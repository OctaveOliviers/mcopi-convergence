Explain-back

The theorem says that tabular initial-visit MC-O-PI converges almost surely to the optimal action-value function even if states are sampled at very unequal and adaptive rates. The crucial symmetry is only within a state: conditional on choosing a state, every action is equally likely to be the initial action. Each state-action pair must nevertheless receive infinite total learning weight. The theorem also assumes finite state/action sets, discounted or proper episodic dynamics, controlled return variance, Robbins–Monro steps, a bounded-factor form of eventual step-size nonincrease, and inertial greedy tie-breaking. It concludes more than convergence of q_k: action-value targets change only finitely often, every sufficiently late selected policy is optimal, and the policy itself eventually freezes when every state has a unique optimal action.

I understand the proof as reducing a noisy value-estimation process to a finite policy-progress argument.

First, the preliminaries establish two fallback facts. Estimates remain bounded because each coordinate can be trapped between two stochastic averages with fixed targets: the smallest and largest q_pi among policies that recur infinitely often. A stochastic average with a genuinely fixed target converges to that target because its squared error has negative expected drift weighted by alpha_k sigma_k(s,a), a summable variance error, and divergent accumulated learning. Hence, if the algorithm ever stops changing its action-value target, q_k converges to that target; a policy occurring infinitely often is then greedy for its own q, so the target must be optimal. Thus a suboptimal constant-target block cannot last forever, and proving only finitely many target changes is enough.

Second, the mean-field calculation explains why action-wise uniformity is the right condition. When two actions at the same state are updated with the same coefficient, the common coefficient can be cancelled/rearranged in the greedy inequality for q_{k+1}. This shows that switching from pi_k(s) to pi_{k+1}(s) cannot lower q_{pi_k} at that state. The Policy Improvement Theorem turns the statewise inequalities into policy improvement. At a target transition the two q-functions differ, so the improvement is strict. Since there are finitely many deterministic policies, a mean-field trajectory can make only finitely many strict improvements; after its target freezes, accumulated learning drives q_k to that target, which must be optimal.

The stochastic proof cannot demand improvement at every transition, so it proves a uniform positive chance of a “successful interval” at every sufficiently late transition. Fix the current target q_pi. A bad pair (s,a) is one whose true value under pi is below the value of pi(s). Its estimated gap d_k compares the estimate for pi(s) with that for a. Before the next target change, equal action-update probabilities make this gap drift toward the strictly positive true gap d_pi. Preventing every bad action from becoming greedy ensures that the next target change satisfies policy improvement.

The positive-gap construction has three stages because neither noise nor sparse state sampling can be ignored. The seed stage requests finitely many favorable updates: raise the reference-action estimate when it is far below target, or lower a competitor otherwise. Either choice increases the relevant gap and preserves the reference action as greedy; inertia then retains it. Each request has a policy-independent positive conditional probability, using uniform action choice and the one-sided return-tail bound. The step-size comparison condition ensures N earlier seed increments create a margin at least proportional to N alpha at that state’s completion time, even if completion occurs much later.

Different states may finish seeding at unrelated times. Conditioning naively on all requested successes could corrupt the later return law at a state that has already finished. The auxiliary probability law avoids that: it reweights only the finitely many requested favorable outcomes. Under the reweighted law all requests succeed, state-selection probabilities remain the same, and ordinary updates at completed states keep their original conditional law. The bounded likelihood ratio later transfers a positive-probability event back to the original law.

The growth stage protects a small seed margin until it reaches the fixed margin delta. While 0<d_k<delta, the true gap is at least 4 delta, so the mean drift contributes at least 3 delta times the state’s accumulated-learning clock. A crossing of zero therefore requires a martingale-noise excursion large enough to defeat both the initial gap and this growing drift. Splitting possible crossings into dyadic ranges of accumulated learning and applying the stopped maximal inequality yields a failure bound proportional to alpha at restart divided by the seed margin. Because the seed margin is proportional to N alpha, choosing N large makes this probability uniformly small.

The lock-in stage starts once the gap reaches delta. The deterministic part of an update cannot push a gap below delta when it is already at least delta, because it moves toward a target gap of at least 4 delta. A later crossing therefore requires a noise excursion of order delta. Square summability of alpha makes the tail probability of such an excursion small at a sufficiently late restart.

Combining the stages and union-bounding over finitely many bad pairs gives fixed p>0 such that a sufficiently late interval either exits the chosen bound K, keeps the same target forever, or ends at a strictly better policy. There can be at most |P|-1 consecutive strict improvements. From any continuing interval, a run of |P| successful intervals therefore has probability at least p^{|P|} and must stop. Repeating this in blocks makes endless target changes before exit have probability zero. Almost-sure boundedness then permits a pathwise deterministic restart after which exit never occurs. Hence there are only finitely many target changes, and the fixed-target argument gives q_k→q_* and eventually optimal greedy policies.

Where the assumptions matter: action-wise uniformity supplies the common within-state coefficient and the positive gap drift; initial-visit updates make the two competing action updates disjoint and their rates exactly the start probabilities; cumulative learning handles arbitrarily rare states; bounded second moments and square-summable steps control noise; the bounded-factor step comparison connects seed size to later protection; inertia keeps unfinished states’ reference actions; finiteness bounds the number of strict policy improvements; discounting/properness makes policy values and return moments usable; boundedness removes the artificial exit time.

Socratic exchange — unassisted answers

### 1. Does the argument require every state's seed or growth stage to finish? Explain finite and infinite endpoints.

No. At a finite endpoint, the argument separates states by their status. A state that completed before the endpoint has each completed gap protected by the growth/lock-in bound. A state still unfinished retains the reference action because every successful seed update only raises that action or lowers a competitor and inertia preserves it during waits. A state that completes exactly at the endpoint is also covered: the seed lemma explicitly gives its positive margin there. Therefore, if the finite endpoint is a target transition before exit, no bad action can be selected and the new policy improves. If the finite endpoint is exit at or before the target change, the interval has already met the separate exit alternative in the progress lemma.

At an infinite endpoint with neither transition nor exit, the target stays equal to q_pi forever inside the bound. That is itself one of the progress lemma's successful alternatives, so no state has to finish seeding or growth. The later fixed-target argument handles this case. This is why the proof says it does not need simultaneous completion—or even completion of every state.

### 2. Why can completed-state return laws be used under the auxiliary measure when future requests depend on earlier returns?

The density process is designed so the future request factors average to one when conditioned on the current or next history. In the appendix's notation, E[L_infinity | F'_{k+1}] = L_{k+1}. Thus for a next-step random variable Y,

widehat E[Y | F'_k] = E[L_{k+1}Y | F'_k]/L_k.

This identity integrates out every later factor, including factors chosen using the current return. At a completed state there is no current request, so the current multiplier L_{k+1}/L_k equals one. Conditional on that state being selected, the displayed formula therefore reduces to the original conditional action-and-return law. The dependence of future requests on the present return does not bias the present completed-state update because those future likelihood factors have conditional mean one and disappear through the tower/martingale identity.

### 3. What permits selecting a late restart after observing the path while K was fixed in advance?

The proof does not apply a stopping-time estimate at a future-dependent restart. For each deterministic integer r≥k_0 it first proves, on a probability-one event, that infinitely many target changes before zeta_r have probability zero. It then intersects these events over the countable set of deterministic integers r. Separately, boundedness—and the fact that K was chosen strictly larger than every policy target norm—implies that almost every path eventually stays inside K, so on that already-intersected probability-one event there exists some deterministic integer value r along the realized path with zeta_r=infinity. The proof may choose that integer pathwise after all deterministic-r conclusions have been established simultaneously. It never conditions at this selected r or claims that the eventual-containment time is a stopping time.
